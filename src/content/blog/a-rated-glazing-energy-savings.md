# A+ Rated Windows: What They Save on Your Heating Bill

Energy labels on windows look reassuringly like the ones on fridges, but the number they translate to on your gas bill is less obvious. This guide explains what a Window Energy Rating actually measures, what Building Regulations Part L 2021 now demands, and what UK homeowners can realistically expect A+ glazing to save each year in 2026.

## What the Window Energy Rating actually measures

The Window Energy Rating, or WER, is administered in the UK by the British Fenestration Rating Council (BFRC) and runs from G at the bottom to A++ at the top. It combines three variables: thermal transmittance (how much heat escapes), solar gain (how much sunlight warms the room), and air leakage (how draught-proof the seals are). A window can score highly by minimising heat loss, by maximising useful solar gain, or, ideally, by doing both.

The number that matters for building regulations, though, is not the letter grade. It is the Uw value, expressed in watts per square metre per kelvin (W/m²K). Uw covers the whole window: frame, glass and edge seal together. The Ug value covers only the glass, and salespeople sometimes quote it because it looks better.

## What Part L 2021 requires on replacements

Approved Document L Volume 1 (Dwellings) 2021 edition tightened the U-value requirement for replacement windows in England from 1.6 to 1.4 W/m²K. Wales followed with its own 2022 update to broadly similar levels. Scotland's Section 6 sits at 1.4 W/m²K for replacements. In practical terms, any 2026 UK window quote should be showing a whole-window Uw of 1.4 or lower, and most reputable manufacturers ship 1.2 to 1.3 as their standard product.

That figure is achievable with a well-specified double glazed unit. Triple glazing is not required by regulation, and in most existing UK properties it is not the most cost-effective upgrade. The bigger returns tend to come from getting the double glazing specification right.

## The spec that actually delivers savings

Two A+ rated windows with the same headline label can differ by ten to twenty per cent on real-world performance. The details below are where the savings hide.

### Warm-edge spacer

The spacer is the strip that separates the two panes of glass around the edge of the sealed unit. Traditional aluminium spacers conduct heat straight through the seal and create cold edges that condensation loves. Warm-edge spacers, typically in Swisspacer, Super Spacer or TGI form, cut edge-of-glass heat loss by up to 65 per cent and are worth insisting on.

### Gas fill

Argon fill between the panes is now the standard. It sits between about 90 and 95 per cent purity when correctly filled and pushes the Ug value down to around 1.1 W/m²K on a typical A+ unit. Krypton is denser and performs slightly better in narrower cavities, but costs materially more and only makes sense in triple glazing or where cavity depth is constrained.

### Low-E soft coat

A low-emissivity coating on the internal pane reflects long-wave heat back into the room. Soft-coat (sputtered) coatings outperform hard-coat (pyrolytic) coatings on U-value and are near-universal on A+ units. If a quote omits the coating specification, that is worth asking about.

### Triple glazing

Triple glazing typically drops Uw to around 0.8 W/m²K. On a UK house already fitted with A+ double glazing, the incremental annual saving from moving to triple is usually £30 to £90, against an incremental cost of £150 to £350 per window. The payback runs to twenty years or more, so triple glazing tends to be justified on comfort and noise grounds rather than pure economics, except in very cold or exposed locations.

## Realistic annual savings by property type

Independent modelling using SAP methodology, applied against 2026 UK average gas prices, gives the following typical savings when replacing G rated (single glazed or old sealed units) with A+ rated double glazing.

| Property type | Heating demand cut | Annual saving 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| One or two-bed flat | 8 to 12% | £70 to £120 |
| Mid-terrace 2 or 3 bed | 12 to 15% | £110 to £180 |
| Semi-detached 3 bed | 14 to 17% | £150 to £260 |
| Detached 4 bed | 15 to 18% | £180 to £340 |
| Large detached with bays | 15 to 20% | £260 to £460 |

These numbers assume roughly 30 per cent of the fabric heat loss was previously through the windows, which is typical of pre-2002 UK stock. If your existing glazing is already C or D rated double glazing, the saving from moving to A+ is roughly a third of these figures.

## Payback period and how to think about it

At today's costs a full-house A+ upgrade typically pays back on pure heating savings over 10 to 15 years. That looks long against a mortgage horizon but short against the 25 to 35 year design life of a modern UPVC or timber composite window. Two forces are moving the payback shorter: energy prices have not returned to pre-2022 levels, and the resale premium on EPC-improved properties has widened.

That said, most homeowners who replace windows do not do so purely on payback maths. They do it because the existing units have failed seals, sticking hinges, drafty rebates or condensation between the panes. Once you are replacing anyway, the incremental cost of A+ over the minimum-compliance specification is small, and the saving compounds for decades.

## The benefits that don't show on the bill

The financial saving is only part of the story, and often not the part homeowners notice first.

- Warmer surfaces on the inside pane, which means less radiant chill sitting near a window on a January evening
- No cold downdraught behind curtains or under sills, so the room feels usable in winter without turning the thermostat up
- Dramatically reduced condensation on the internal glass, particularly with warm-edge spacers, which cuts the black mould that follows persistent condensation
- Noise reduction of 5 to 8 dB from a typical A+ double glazed unit over an old single glazed one, more with asymmetric pane thicknesses
- Improved security ratings, since PAS 24 hardware and laminated glass options are standard on A+ product lines

These day-to-day gains are what homeowners tend to notice within the first week. The bill savings arrive in the winter that follows.

## When triple glazing is genuinely worth it

Triple glazing earns its cost in three specific situations. First, on north-facing elevations in exposed rural or coastal locations, where the wind chill drives up losses even with good double glazing. Second, on rooms occupied for long periods (home offices, bedrooms of light sleepers, nurseries) where the comfort and acoustic gains are valued in themselves. Third, on new-build extensions and knock-throughs targeting an EPC A rating, where the whole-house SAP calculation benefits from every fabric improvement.

For a straight like-for-like UK window replacement on an existing home, A+ double glazing at Uw 1.2 W/m²K remains the best-value specification in 2026.

## Quick self-check before you commission a quote

Before you compare installer proposals, know where you are starting from and what you are targeting.

- Read the U-value line on your current EPC (windows section) to see your baseline
- Check the year of your current glazing: pre-2002 is almost certainly single or non-argon double
- Confirm your postcode's typical gas unit rate (Ofgem's price cap page is the neutral source)
- Decide whether comfort and condensation, or pure bill savings, is the primary driver
- Note any rooms where noise is a real issue and flag them for asymmetric or acoustic glazing

## Common labelling traps to watch for

The retail glazing market is not always careful with the difference between Ug and Uw. A quote that proudly lists a U-value of 1.0 W/m²K is usually citing the centre-pane Ug, which excludes the frame and edge seal. The whole-window Uw is almost always higher, typically by 0.2 to 0.4 W/m²K depending on the frame system. Similarly, an "A rated" label without the plus is the minimum for compliance in 2026 but not the target. Ask for the WER certificate or the BFRC label reference for the exact product line quoted, not a generic range brochure. If the salesperson cannot provide it in writing at the point of quote, treat that as a specification you cannot yet verify, and adjust the price you are willing to sign for accordingly.
